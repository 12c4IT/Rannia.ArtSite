## What the cultural frame is

The cultural frame reads a work through **the social conditions in which it was made and received**: class, race, gender, ethnicity, sexuality, religion, politics, and the historical moment. It treats art as never separable from the world that produced it, and asks how meaning is negotiated between artist, work, and society.

## Questions this frame asks

- What social, political, or historical conditions produced this work?
- Whose voice is speaking, and whose voice is absent?
- What cultural values does the work embody, contest, or transform?
- Who is the audience — and who is excluded?

## Worked example — Bourgeois' *Maman* (1999)

**Question**: Explain how the cultural frame reads Louise Bourgeois' *Maman* (1999).

Read culturally, *Maman* sits at the end of the twentieth century's re-evaluation of the maternal body in art. For most of the modernist canon, "the mother" appeared either as sentimental Madonna or as absent-cause of male genius. Bourgeois refused both. Her mother, Joséphine, enters the work as *labour* (Joséphine restored tapestries — a slow, patient craft), as *skill*, and as a body with agency. The choice of bronze — a material historically used for civic monuments to male authority — places the maternal at the scale of the civic, not the domestic.

The public siting of *Maman* is itself a cultural argument. When the first version was installed at Tate Modern's opening in 2000, Bourgeois placed the maternal — a subject long confined to the private sphere — at the physical centre of one of Europe's most public civic spaces. Feminist criticism has read this as a deliberate reversal of the modernist gendering of scale: monumentality is here reclaimed for the maternal, not surrendered to it.

**Marker's note**: The cultural frame is not "the artist's biography". It is the reading of the work in its social and historical conditions — what the work argues *against* or *with* in its wider culture. This response earns the mark by naming what *Maman* is arguing against (the modernist gendering of monumentality) and what it is arguing for (the maternal as public content), not just describing the mother-and-child theme.
