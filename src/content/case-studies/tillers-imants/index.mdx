      Imants Tillers's engagement with cultural context through appropriation is genuine, formally consistent, and — in the case of *The Nine Shots* (1985) — significantly and importantly contested. To evaluate his practice fairly, both the formal ambition and the political consequence have to be named.

      Read at the level of the formal method, Tillers's appropriation strategy is sustained and coherent. The canvasboard grid is a container designed to hold visual quotation from multiple traditions simultaneously — Baselitz's inverted figures, Kiefer's textual painting, Clemente's saturated figuration. The formal register is postmodern in the strictest sense: pastiche without a stable original, quotation as method rather than as reference. Judged as a formal system for holding cultural material, the practice is one of the most rigorously worked-out in international postmodern painting.

      Read against the specifically Australian cultural context, however, the same appropriation method operates asymmetrically. Tillers's 1985 quotation of Michael Nelson Jagamara's Papunya imagery was named by Jagamara himself and by other Aboriginal artists as a taking of imagery Tillers had no cultural authority to use. The formal method that reads as innocent pastiche when applied to European sources becomes cultural taking when applied to Aboriginal imagery, because the two source traditions are not culturally symmetrical. To evaluate Tillers's engagement with cultural context therefore requires holding two judgements at once: the practice is a serious international postmodern painting project, AND that project made a specific mistake whose consequence is part of the work's meaning. The 2001 collaborative paintings with Jagamara are Tillers's own subsequent engagement with that history; whether they resolve it, complicate it, or both is a genuine open question.

## Artist practice

{/* AI DRAFT — Rannia to rewrite. */}

Imants Tillers has been painting on standard commercial canvasboards since 1981, numbering each panel sequentially across his entire lifetime output. As of the mid-2020s the count runs into six figures. Each finished work is an assembly of a numbered range of these panels — *The Nine Shots* comprises panels 5,286 to 5,376, *Diaspora* comprises 19,161 to 19,448. The numbering is not incidental; the panels themselves are the practice, and the assembled works are episodes within it.
