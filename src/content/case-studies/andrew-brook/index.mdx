      Brook Andrew's *Sexy and dangerous* (1996) uses appropriation in a way that the postmodern frame explains partially but incompletely. Read postmodernly, the work quotes a 19th-century ethnographic photograph without permission and without softening — a textbook appropriation gesture. The neon overlay of the words "sexy" and "dangerous" collides two visual economies (colonial-documentary and contemporary-commercial) in exactly the way a postmodern reading anticipates: pastiche, quotation, refusal of the original's authority.

      What the postmodern frame does *not* fully explain is the political specificity of the appropriation. Andrew is a Wiradjuri artist appropriating a colonial image made *of* Aboriginal people *by* white institutions. The move is not stylistic quotation; it is the seizure of an image that never belonged to its subject, by a descendant of that subject's community. To read *Sexy and dangerous* only through the postmodern frame is to name the formal move (appropriation) without naming what makes this particular appropriation matter (the return of an image to a community from whom it was taken).

Postmodern gives us the "how" (the appropriation, the pastiche, the multi-register collision); cultural gives us the "why" (the specific colonial archive being reoccupied, the specific Wiradjuri claim being made). To the extent that the postmodern frame explains Andrew's use of appropriation, it explains half of what the work is doing.

## Artist practice



Brook Andrew's practice threads across media — photography, print, sculpture, neon, video, curatorial work — but is unified by a sustained investigation of colonial visual archives and the ways contemporary institutional space can be used to reactivate them. His directorship of the 2020 Sydney Biennale (*NIRIN*) was itself a curatorial argument: that First Nations perspectives are not a "content area" to be included in international contemporary art but a framework through which international contemporary art can be organised.
