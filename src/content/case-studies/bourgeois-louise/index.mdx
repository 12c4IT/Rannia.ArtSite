    frames:
      subjective: |
        Read subjectively, Maman is not an insect. It is a portrait — of Louise Bourgeois' mother, Joséphine, who died when the artist was twenty-one and whose absence haunted her practice for the next seventy years. Bourgeois told interviewers repeatedly that the spider was her mother, praising her mother's cleverness, patience, and protective instinct.[^bernadac-obrist-1998]

        The subjective reading turns on the *feeling* the work produces in a viewer standing beneath it. It is a maternal architecture: enormous, watchful, sheltering. The viewer occupies the position of a small child looking up at an adult body. That transposition of scale is the emotional engine of the work — not the spider's biological accuracy but the way it makes the adult viewer feel, briefly, like a child.

        Bourgeois' own psychoanalysis (she was in analysis for over thirty years[^storr-2003]) is inseparable from this reading. The Maman series is not simply *about* her mother; it is a working-through — the artist making, in monumental permanent bronze, the maternal presence that death had made permanent absence.

      cultural: |
        Read culturally, Maman sits at the end of the twentieth century's re-evaluation of the maternal body in art. For most of the modernist canon, "the mother" appeared either as sentimental Madonna or as absent-cause of male genius. Bourgeois refused both. Her mother enters the work as *labour*, as *skill* (Joséphine restored tapestries — a slow, patient craft that Bourgeois compared to weaving), and as a body with agency.[^bernadac-obrist-1998]

        The public siting of Maman is itself a cultural argument. When the first version was installed at Tate Modern's Turbine Hall for the museum's opening in May 2000{/* TODO: verify exact year of Turbine Hall commission — Bourgeois' Turbine Hall commission included "I Do, I Undo, I Redo" (three towers) alongside a spider; check whether "Maman" specifically was the 1999 or 2000 Tate Modern installation */}, Bourgeois placed the maternal — a subject long confined to the domestic and the private — at the physical centre of one of Europe's most public new civic spaces. Feminist criticism has read this as a deliberate reversal of the modernist gendering of scale: monumentality is here reclaimed for the maternal, not surrendered to it.

      structural: |
        Structurally, Maman resolves an old sculptural problem: how to make a heavy material feel weightless. The choice of bronze is orthodox — bronze has been the material of public monuments for millennia. What Bourgeois does with it is not. The legs are elongated far past what bronze normally needs, tapered to points that read almost as calligraphic marks against the ground plane. The result is a sculpture that occupies enormous volume while touching the earth at only eight points.

        The egg sac introduces the second material — a stainless steel mesh cage holding twenty-six polished marble eggs. Marble against bronze against steel: three distinct material vocabularies compressed into a single work. The whiteness of the eggs, the darkness of the bronze, and the industrial grey of the mesh set up a chromatic hierarchy that resolves at the sculpture's most tender point.

        Scale, again, is a formal device. The spider is not merely large — it is the exact size at which an adult viewer must physically position themselves beneath it to see the eggs. The work choreographs the viewer's body. Formalism here is not decoration; it is the way the sculpture *works on* the body of the audience.

      postmodern: |
        Read through the postmodern frame, Maman is a knowing citation. Every monumental bronze animal in Western public art history stands behind it — from Rome's Capitoline she-wolf to the many bronze lions guarding civic buildings. Bourgeois quotes the tradition and inverts it. The animal is not male; it is not heroic; it is not a symbol of civic power. It is a mother, and its power is generative rather than martial.

        The multiplicity of casts is itself a postmodern gesture. Copies exist in Tate Modern, Guggenheim Bilbao, Ottawa, Bilbao, Tokyo, Seoul, Doha, and Kansas City{/* TODO: Rannia to confirm the current cast locations — six was the original edition but there are also artist's proofs */}. There is no *original* Maman. The work exists as a distributed presence — each cast the "same" work, each cast in a different city, none more authentic than the others. Bourgeois plays with — and against — the modernist cult of the unique original.

    notes: |
      Maman is one of the most-cited sculptures of the past thirty years. It is worth pausing on why: not because it is technically unprecedented (bronze casting is ancient), but because it collapses the modernist separation between private feeling and public form. That collapse is what makes it repeatedly useful in an HSC response — it can anchor readings across every one of the four frames.

      subjective: |
        The subjective reading of *The Destruction of the Father* is the one Bourgeois herself invited. She published the story behind the work in 1974, describing it as an imagined childhood revenge: at the dinner table each night her father would hold court, pontificating and belittling, until one evening the children (in her fantasy) rose up, killed him, cooked him, and consumed him.[^bernadac-obrist-1998]

        Read this way, the work is a bounded psychological event — a childhood fantasy of parricide made material. The red light is not literal; it is the colour a child imagines the inside of a body to be. The forms are not organs; they are what an imagined act of destruction *feels* like from inside a young body that has no vocabulary yet for its own anger.

        Bourgeois famously said that art is a guarantee of sanity{/* TODO: verify Bourgeois quote "art is a guarantee of sanity" — widely attributed, likely from the Bernadac/Obrist writings, needs page reference */}. This work is what she meant. The point is not that art *depicts* trauma; it is that art can *contain* what would otherwise be unbearable.

      cultural: |
        Read culturally, the work is a document of a very particular kind of mid-twentieth-century family: the bourgeois patriarch, dominant at the dinner table, whose authority extended over wife, children, mistress (who in Bourgeois' case lived in the family home as the children's English tutor). The Destruction of the Father is not just Louise Bourgeois' father; it is *the* father — the figure whose social power second-wave feminism was in the process of naming when the work was made in 1974.

        The date matters. 1974 sits inside the peak years of second-wave feminism in North America. Bourgeois was not a member of any political movement, but *The Destruction of the Father* enters the conversation those movements were having about domestic power. Read through the cultural frame, the work is a private image doing public political work — even if Bourgeois herself would resist that framing.

      structural: |
        Structurally, The Destruction of the Father is one of the most radical installations of its decade. Bourgeois works with latex — a material more associated with medical prosthetics than fine art — and casts it in biomorphic shapes that resist identification. Are they mounds of food? Breasts? Teeth? Organs? The refusal to resolve is the point.

        The red light does something crucial: it eliminates local colour. Everything reads as one warm-toned continuum. The installation becomes a single environmental body that the viewer physically enters. This is not sculpture in the Renaissance sense (an object to be walked around); it is sculpture as room, as enveloping condition. Bourgeois anticipates the immersive installations of the 1990s by two decades.

        Scale is also a structural argument. The work is bigger than a table but smaller than a room. It is domestic-sized — the exact scale at which a family scene would happen. The formal choice keeps the fantasy legible as a *family* fantasy, not a public monument.

      postmodern: |
        Postmodern readings foreground the work's refusal of a stable meaning. Is *The Destruction of the Father* about her actual father? A universal father? The father as a psychoanalytic concept? Bourgeois' own writings shift the answer. The work sits at the point where autobiography becomes myth, and myth becomes theory.

        The blurring of high and low is also postmodern. Latex is a low material — medical, prosthetic, industrial. Bourgeois uses it at the scale of monumental fine-art installation. The formal register is impossible to place: the work is neither the pure abstraction her minimalist contemporaries were making, nor the figurative work of the pre-modern tradition. It occupies a category that did not yet exist when it was made.

    notes: |
      Bourgeois taught that "the subject" of a work can be a table, and the table can hold a life[^bernadac-obrist-1998]. This is the artwork where that principle is most literally staged. Pair it with Maman and you have a practice in which the two most tyrannical figures of Bourgeois' childhood — the father and the mother — are both given monumental sculptural form. The father is destroyed; the mother is memorialised. The same practice contains both.

Younger audiences — feminist critics, students, teenage viewers finding Maman in a public plaza — often meet the work with immediate emotional recognition, before they read the biography. # Artist practice

Louise Bourgeois' practice is one of the twentieth century's most sustained meditations on the relationship between private psychological material and public sculptural form. Across seventy years — from her arrival in New York in 1938 until her death in 2010 — she refused the modernist separation between the artist's life and the artist's work. Her practice does not *illustrate* biography; it uses biography as raw material.

## Formation

Bourgeois was born in Paris in 1911.[^bernadac-obrist-1998] Her parents ran a workshop that restored antique tapestries — the mother, Joséphine, was the skilled restorer; the father, Louis, handled the business. From childhood, Bourgeois was surrounded by the slow, patient work of textile repair — a domain she later credited as the origin of her interest in sewing, weaving, and the fabric assemblages she would return to in her nineties.

Her mother died in 1932, when Bourgeois was twenty-one. The loss shaped everything that followed. Bourgeois has said in interview that art was, for her, "a guarantee of sanity"{/* TODO: verify the exact form and source of this quote */}, and the phrase reads directly against her mother's death: the practice as a container for grief that would otherwise have no shape.

She trained at the Sorbonne (studying mathematics before shifting to art) and worked briefly in the studios of Fernand Léger and other Parisian figures.[^storr-2003] In 1938 she married the American art historian Robert Goldwater and moved with him to New York. She would not return to Paris to live.

## Working in New York

The New York art world Bourgeois entered was hostile territory for a European-trained woman working with figurative and biomorphic form. The dominant idiom shifted from abstract expressionism through minimalism to conceptualism across her middle decades, and Bourgeois was consistently out of step with each. She continued making — sculpture, drawings, prints, writings — in relative obscurity until the late 1970s.

The turning point was the 1982 MoMA retrospective, curated by Deborah Wye. Bourgeois was seventy-one. She was the first woman to receive a full MoMA retrospective.[^moma-1982] From that point until her death in 2010, her recognition grew steadily, and by the 1990s she was one of the most-cited living artists in international contemporary art.

## Recurring concerns

Three concerns run through the practice:

**The maternal body.** From the earliest wooden "Personages" of the late 1940s (which Bourgeois described as "presences" of absent family members) through the monumental Spider works of the 1990s and 2000s, the mother appears as protector, as absence, as craft, as body. Bourgeois' mother — Joséphine, the tapestry restorer — is arguably the single most important figure in the entire practice.

**Paternal authority.** The father appears as antagonist, as tyrant, and — in *The Destruction of the Father* (1974) — as the subject of imagined revenge. Bourgeois described her father, Louis, as domineering, unfaithful, and cruel, and the practice returns to the figure repeatedly.

**Memory as material.** Bourgeois worked *in* memory, not *about* it. The Cell series (from the late 1980s onward) — architectural units the viewer looks into rather than around — treats memory as a physical space that can be entered. She is not depicting remembered scenes; she is building rooms that hold the affective texture of remembering.

## Materials and process

Bourgeois worked across almost every sculptural medium in twentieth-century Western practice: bronze, marble, latex, plaster, wood, fabric, glass, found domestic objects, and her own clothing. Her practice is not defined by a single signature material. What unifies it is a relationship *between* materials — the collision of soft against hard, industrial against domestic, permanent against fugitive.

In the late works, she often used her own clothes — dresses she had kept since her twenties — cut, stitched, and assembled into new sculptures. That gesture crystallises the whole practice: the artist's own life, physically incorporated into the material of the work.
