      Anish Kapoor's *Cloud Gate* (2004) uses the structural frame — the frame that reads for material, surface, scale, and formal engineering — as its primary mode of argument. The work is 168 stainless-steel plates polished to a seamless mirror finish, engineered so that the viewer cannot see the joins between plates from ordinary viewing distances. The formal decision *is* the meaning: the sculpture argues that a surface can be sufficiently continuous to dissolve into pure reflection, and it makes this argument through the physical fact of the surface.

At that scale the mirrored surface reflects an entire city skyline; at gallery scale it would reflect only the room it stood in. The structural argument (a continuous mirror produces a phenomenal condition) is scaled to the situation where it does the most work — a public plaza where thousands of people move under it daily.

## Artist practice



Anish Kapoor works across scales that would ordinarily require different studios and different disciplines — from small pigment sculptures held in the palm to civic commissions the size of a building. What unifies the practice is a sustained investigation of what sculpture can produce phenomenologically: not what it represents, but what it does to the viewer's perception in the room. His industrial-scale collaborations with fabricators and engineers (Cecil Balmond on the *Marsyas* PVC membrane at Tate Modern, structural teams on the Millennium Park commission) are integral to the practice, not incidental to it.
