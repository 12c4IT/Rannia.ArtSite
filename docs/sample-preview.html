Louise Bourgeois

French-American · 1911–2010

Across a seventy-year practice, Bourgeois turned private trauma — her mother's death, her father's betrayal — into monumental public form, refusing the modernist separation of life and work.

01 — Conceptual Framework

The four agencies, summarised

Artist

Bourgeois

Born Paris 1911; trained in mathematics at the Sorbonne before turning to art under Léger and others. Her family ran a tapestry restoration workshop — an environment of skilled hand-labour that recurs across her work.

Two formative events shape the practice: her mother's death in 1932, and the long, openly conducted affair her father carried on with the family's live-in English tutor through Louise's childhood.

Artwork

Seven decades, one lexicon

Sculpture, installation, drawing, fabric, print and writing across seventy years. A recurring vocabulary — the spider, the spiral, the cell, the fragmented body — operates as a personal iconography learned across works.

Materials are pointedly chosen: bronze, marble and steel for the public sculptures; latex, plaster, fabric and found objects for the more intimate work.

World

The whole twentieth century

Two world wars; the rise and fall of European modernism; displacement to the United States in 1938; the post-war New York art world's successive dominant modes; second-wave feminism; the late-century institutional re-evaluation of women artists.

Bourgeois aligned with none of the dominant movements. Her work proceeds as if from one side.

Audience

An audience that finally arrived

Four decades of relative obscurity. Her 1982 MoMA retrospective — the institution's first for a woman — marked the turning point. The 1990s and 2000s saw her become one of the most exhibited living artists in the world.

Maman's installation at the opening of Tate Modern's Turbine Hall in 2000 is the marker of this shift.

02 — Artist Practice

A small territory, repeatedly worked

Bourgeois's practice is a sustained inquiry into a small number of psychological territories — the maternal, the paternal, the body, the home, memory — pursued across seven decades and at every available scale, from the page-sized drawing to the building-sized sculpture. The territories do not change. The forms do.

Materials as language

Bourgeois worked with whatever the work required: marble carved by hand or by assistants in Italy; bronze cast in New York foundries; latex poured in her Brooklyn studio; fabric stitched from her own discarded clothing in the final two decades of her life. She rejected the modernist hierarchy of materials — the sense that bronze is serious and fabric is decorative — and instead let each material carry the psychological content it could carry bestverify. Latex sags and pools like flesh. Marble cradles like a held thing. Steel makes the impossible structure possible. The choice is the content.

Recurring forms

Across the practice, a small lexicon of forms returns: the spider (maternal, weaving, protective, threatening); the spiral (compulsion, return, the impossibility of moving on); the cell (containment, the interior of a self or a family); the fragmented body (the limb, the breast, the figure missing a head). These are not symbols in a one-to-one code. They are sites at which Bourgeois worked her territory, repeatedly, the same questions asked of different forms.

The late practice

In the final two decades — roughly 1990 to 2010 — Bourgeois worked at the largest scale of her career, producing the Cell installations, the Maman / Spider series, and a body of late fabric work made from clothing. The late work is often read as a summing-up. It is more accurate to say it is the moment when her audience and her resources finally permitted the scale she had been working toward all along.

03 — Artwork one

Maman, 1999

Bourgeois made the Maman sculptures explicit acts of mourning and tribute. In a written statement issued with the work she described the spider as "an ode to my mother. She was my best friend. Like a spider, my mother was a weaver" — drawing the analogy from her family's tapestry restoration workshop in Choisy-le-Roi, where she had assisted from childhoodverify.

The subjective reading turns on this transposition of grief into form. The spider is at once protective — the marble sac of eggs cradled beneath her abdomen — and threatening, her thin steel legs creating an enclosure the viewer cannot avoid walking under. Bourgeois has been candid across decades of interviews that her practice is autobiographical and reparative: a way of returning to her mother's death in 1932, when Louise was twenty-one, and to the unresolved grief that followedverify.

The work refuses the modernist convention that a sculpture's meaning is given by its formal properties alone. It asks to be read alongside her biography.

Maman arrived at a particular moment in late-twentieth-century art. The 1990s saw a critical re-evaluation of women artists Bourgeois's age who had worked outside the modernist canon for decades — Agnes Martin, Eva Hesse, Lee Bontecou — and Bourgeois became the most visible beneficiary. Her 1982 MoMA retrospective, the institution's first for a woman, marked the inflection pointverify.

Read culturally, the spider is also a refusal of the post-war American sculptural orthodoxies — the minimalist grid, the male artist as cool fabricator — that had dominated the institutions while she made her work privately for half a century. Maman is unembarrassedly figurative, unembarrassedly emotional, unembarrassedly maternal. It insists that the maternal is a subject worthy of monumental scale.

The work has been read through feminist art history as an act of late-career reclamation: of her own practice, and of the categories the post-war canon excludedverify.

The structural reading attends to scale, material and engineering. Maman is built at the threshold of architecture rather than object: at approximately 9.3 metres on the longest axis, she is large enough to occupy the viewer's field of vision and to create a space beneath. Bourgeois exploits this scale: the viewer is forced into the position of a small thing under a large protective/predatory creature, and the experience is bodily before it is intellectual.

The material vocabulary is precise. The bronze body and legs are visibly cast, with weld seams and patina left legible — Bourgeois rejected the modernist preference for the seamless object. The marble of the egg sac is in tension with the bronze: organic against industrial, soft against hard, cool against warm. The stainless steel of the supporting structure is functional and clinical, the engineering that makes the impossible animal stand.

The composition is asymmetric and unstable. Although the spider has eight legs, no two read alike: each is differently angled, differently weighted. The work cannot be circled and resolved.

Maman exists in multiple editions sited at major museums across continents — Tate, National Gallery of Canada, Mori Art Museum, Crystal Bridges, Bilbao. This proliferation is itself postmodern: the work refuses the modernist insistence on the unique object, instead operating as a recurring motif whose meaning shifts with each location and each photographing.

The work also resists the modernist purity of medium. Maman is sculpture and installation and site-marker and photograph (the Tate Modern Turbine Hall opening images of 2000 are inseparable from the work's reception). The Spider series spans drawing, fabric, bronze, found-object cells. The motif is not bound to one form.

Postmodernism's interest in the artist's biographical entry into the work — Cindy Sherman's masquerade, Tracey Emin's bed — finds an older relative in Bourgeois. She made autobiographical practice respectable inside the institution long before the 1990s caught up.

04 — Artwork two

The Destruction of the Father, 1974

The Destruction of the Father is Bourgeois's most explicit confrontation with the patriarchal violence she identified at the centre of her childhood. She has described the work as the staging of a fantasy: the family children dismembering and consuming the domineering father at the dinner tableverify.

Read subjectively, the work is a primal scene made room-sized. The red light, the wet-looking latex forms, the table-as-altar, the cave-like surround — all of it constructs the dream's interior rather than its exterior. The viewer enters the work physically; they are inside the fantasy, not looking at it from outside.

The work's title is also its argument. The father is destroyed not in the world but in the work, repeatedly, ritually, by being made into a piece of art that can be entered and circled and looked at by strangers. The catharsis is the making.

Made in 1974, The Destruction of the Father arrived alongside second-wave feminism's project of bringing the private — the kitchen, the bedroom, the family — into political view. The dinner table as site of male power was a recognised feminist theme; Judy Chicago's The Dinner Party (1974–79) was in production simultaneously.

Bourgeois's contribution was to refuse the celebratory turn of much feminist art of the period in favour of something darker. Her work is not a recovered women's history. It is a staging of patriarchal violence with the daughter as both victim and avenger. Read culturally, it inserts a Freudian-influenced personal mythology into the feminist discourse — the work owes as much to psychoanalysis as to political feminismverify.

The work's materials are central to its meaning. Latex — soft, organic, bodily, capable of sagging and pooling — operates against the architectural plaster and wood of the supporting structure. The red light unifies the field optically and emotionally: every surface is bathed in the same wavelength, collapsing object and ground into a single sensory experience.

The composition is theatrical. A central table-form is flanked by symmetrical clusters of bulbous shapes, the arrangement reading equally as a Last Supper and an operating theatre. Scale is deliberately ambiguous: the forms are too large to be food and too small to be people, occupying a dream-logic register where size is psychological rather than literal.

The viewer's relationship to the work is also structurally controlled. Bourgeois built it as a walk-in tableau rather than an object to be circled, so the work begins with the architectural decision of an entrance and a directed line of sight.

The Destruction of the Father is a tableau, a sculptural genre with deep pre-modern roots — Christian Stations of the Cross, baroque grottoes, the dioramas of natural history museums. By picking up that genre in 1974, Bourgeois sidesteps modernism's abstract object-art and aligns with a tradition modernism had dismissed.

The work is also narrative — it tells a story, asks the viewer to read it, refuses the modernist injunction against literary content in art. This pre-modernist move turns out to be sharply contemporary: by the 1980s, narrative tableaux would be everywhere (Mike Kelley, Jeff Wall, Cindy Sherman's tableaux photographs).

Read postmodernly, Bourgeois's refusal to choose between sculpture, theatre and psychoanalytic illustration is the work's defining gesture.
